Our Mission

TPOTDOC… What?

TPOTDOC stands for The Principles Of The Doctrine Of Christ: the six foundation stones every new believer builds on, named in Hebrews 6:1–2.

Thank you for visiting this website! What a funny name, right? Well, I just wanted to let you know why the site is called TPOTDOC.

It’s based on a verse in the Bible, found in Hebrews 6:1–2. In that verse, the writer is talking about moving beyond these principles, which are also referred to as foundational. As I kept praying about building this reference site for new believers who are just coming to Christ, the Holy Spirit showed me these items in a new light, and that these are the best starting foundations for our faith in Christ.

— Yv'e Bridges

Foundation Stone 1 · Word Study

What is repentance?

Repentance means changing your mind about sin and about God, and letting that change turn your whole life around.

The New Testament was first written in Greek. The word translated “repentance” is metanoia. It is built from two smaller words: meta, which carries the idea of “after” or “change,” and noeō, which means “to think” or “to understand.” Put together, it means to think differently afterward: to reconsider.

Three things happen when we repent

  • We see it differently. God opens our eyes, and we agree with Him that sin is wrong.
  • We feel it. The old word for this is compunction: a real, godly sorrow over sin. It is not shame that crushes us. It is sorrow that leads us to God.
  • We turn around. Repentance is a reversal of direction. We stop walking away from God and start walking with Him.

Fruit that fits repentance

John the Baptist preached to the Pharisees and Sadducees. These were religious people who trusted their birthright, their family line, and their traditions to save them. John told them that real repentance shows up in how we live.

That fruit is not something we produce by trying harder. It is what the Holy Spirit grows in us:

Recommended reading: Matthew 3:1–10, and Galatians 5:4–26, where the apostle Paul writes to people trying to be made right with God by keeping the law.

What are “dead works”?

Hebrews calls this “repentance from dead works.” Dead works are anything that cannot give life: the sins we used to live in, and also our own efforts to earn God’s approval. We turn away from both. Our new life comes from Jesus, not from trying harder.

The good news

Repentance is not a punishment. It is a doorway. When we turn to God, He forgives and He refreshes.

Foundation Stone 2 · Word Study

What is faith toward God?

Faith means being persuaded that God is true, and leaning your whole weight on Jesus to save you.

The Greek word for faith is pistis. It comes from a verb that means “to persuade.” So faith begins when God convinces your heart that He is telling the truth.

Four sides of faith

  • Conviction. You are persuaded that God and His Word are true.
  • Reliance. You trust Christ, and Christ alone, for your salvation.
  • Constancy. You keep holding on, day after day.
  • The faith. The word can also mean the gospel itself: the truth we believe.

What did Jesus say about faith?

A Roman officer asked Jesus to heal his servant, and said Jesus only needed to speak the word. Jesus was amazed.

A woman who had been sick for twelve years believed that if she could only touch His garment, she would be healed.

God has given every believer “the measure of faith” (Romans 12:3).

Foundation Stone 3 · Hebrew Roots

What did baptism mean to the Jewish people in Jesus’ day?

Baptism was not a Christian invention. In the time of Jesus and John the Baptist, full-body immersion in water was already a deep part of Jewish life, a way of being made clean and starting over before God.

In Hebrew this immersion is called tevilah. It took place in a pool built for the purpose, called a mikveh, or in natural “living waters” like rivers and lakes.

Even the word in Hebrews 6:2 points back to this. “The doctrine of baptisms” uses the Greek word baptismos (G909), a washing. The same word is used for the “divers washings” of the Old Testament law.

How did the Jewish people understand immersion?

  • Being made clean. The Torah, in books like Leviticus, taught that water moved a person from being ritually unclean (tamei) to clean (tahor). Priests, pilgrims and everyday Jews immersed before entering the Temple, handling holy offerings, or keeping the feasts.
  • A new birth for converts. When a Gentile became a Jew, he was circumcised, offered a sacrifice, and was fully immersed. The rabbis spoke of the convert coming up out of the water like a newborn coming from the womb.
  • Turning back to God. Immersion was more than getting clean on the outside. It was a way of setting yourself apart, turning from the old ways, and drawing close to God.
  • Getting ready for God’s kingdom. Some groups, like the Essenes at Qumran, washed often as a sign of repentance. It showed they were separating from corruption and getting ready for the coming of God’s kingdom.

How does John the Baptist’s baptism fit in?

A call for the whole nation to repent. John drew on what his people already knew about being made clean and starting over. But his baptism was different. Temple washings were repeated again and again, and convert immersion was for Gentiles. John called all of Israel to repent and get right with God, because the Messiah and His kingdom were about to arrive.

Something they already understood. Because the people knew what immersion meant, nobody needed a brand-new explanation when John began baptizing in the Jordan River. They came out to him by the crowds.

John also told them that Someone greater was coming, with a greater baptism:

John’s baptism in water prepared the people. Jesus would baptize with the Holy Ghost, and that promise came true on the day of Pentecost:

Recommended reading: Matthew 3, Mark 1:1–11, Luke 3:1–22, and John 3:22–23.

What does “baptized for the dead” mean?

In 1 Corinthians 15, Paul writes the Bible’s great chapter on the resurrection. In the middle of it he mentions people who were “baptized for the dead”:

Paul was not teaching people to be baptized for someone who has died. Some in Corinth were doing this, and Paul used their own practice to make his point: why would anyone do this unless they believed the dead will rise again? Notice that Paul says “they,” not “we.” He is not making it a doctrine. He is showing how much the resurrection of the dead matters. If the dead do not rise, the practice means nothing, and so does our faith (1 Corinthians 15:17).

Salvation is for the living, by faith in Jesus. Each of us must believe for ourselves: “now is the accepted time; behold, now is the day of salvation” (2 Corinthians 6:2). Read more in Foundation Stone 5: Resurrection.

Foundation Stone 4 · Word Study

What is the laying on of hands?

The laying on of hands is placing your hands on a person while you pray, to bless them, to set them apart for God’s work, or to ask God to heal them or fill them with His Spirit.

Where does the Bible first show it?

The first time is in Genesis 48. Jacob, also called Israel, was old and near death. Joseph brought his two sons to be blessed. Jacob crossed his arms on purpose and put his right hand, the hand of the greater blessing, on the younger son, Ephraim.

Three ways it was used in the Old Testament

In Hebrew this practice is called semikhah, from samak (H5564), “to lean upon.” The one laying on hands leaned his weight on the other.

  • To pass on a blessing. Jacob blessed his grandsons and spoke over their future (Genesis 48).
  • To place sin on a sacrifice. The worshipper laid his hand on the head of the animal offered for him. His sin was counted on the substitute, which died in his place (Leviticus 1:4; 16:21).
  • To give authority for God’s work. God told Moses to lay his hands on Joshua, to make him the next leader of Israel (Numbers 27:18–23).

The sacrifices pointed to Jesus. On the cross, God laid our sin on Him, the perfect Lamb:

How did Jesus and the apostles lay on hands?

  • To bless. “And he took them up in his arms, put his hands upon them, and blessed them” (Mark 10:16).
  • To heal. “He laid his hands on every one of them, and healed them” (Luke 4:40). Jesus said of those who believe, “they shall lay hands on the sick, and they shall recover” (Mark 16:18).
  • To receive the Holy Ghost. “Then laid they their hands on them, and they received the Holy Ghost” (Acts 8:17).
  • To send people out for ministry. “And when they had fasted and prayed, and laid their hands on them, they sent them away” (Acts 13:3).

Because it is so serious, Paul told Timothy not to rush it: “Lay hands suddenly on no man” (1 Timothy 5:22).

Recommended reading: Genesis 48, Numbers 27:15–23, Acts 6:1–7, and Acts 8:14–17.

Foundation Stone 5 · Word Study

What is the resurrection of the dead?

The resurrection of the dead is God’s promise that the bodies of the dead will be raised to life again, just as Jesus rose bodily from the grave.

The resurrection is not a small detail. It is where God’s whole plan of salvation is headed.

Did believers in the Old Testament hope for it?

Yes. Long before Jesus came, God’s people believed He would raise the dead.

Jesus rose first

The resurrection of Jesus is the center of the Christian faith. He rose with a real body. He showed His disciples His hands and feet, and He ate with them.

More than 500 people saw Him alive at one time (1 Corinthians 15:3–8). Because He rose, everyone who belongs to Him will rise too.

People God raised back to life

The Bible tells of people who were raised from the dead. Each one shows God’s power over death:

  • Elijah and the widow’s son (1 Kings 17:17–24)
  • Elisha and the Shunammite woman’s son (2 Kings 4:32–37)
  • A man who touched Elisha’s bones (2 Kings 13:21)
  • The widow’s son at Nain (Luke 7:11–15)
  • Jairus’ daughter (Luke 8:49–56)
  • Lazarus, four days in the tomb (John 11:38–44)
  • Saints who came out of their graves after Jesus rose (Matthew 27:52–53)
  • Tabitha, also called Dorcas (Acts 9:36–42)
  • Eutychus, who fell from a window (Acts 20:9–12)

These people later died again. Jesus is different: “Christ being raised from the dead dieth no more; death hath no more dominion over him” (Romans 6:9).

Foundation Stone 6 · Word Study

What is eternal judgment?

Eternal judgment is the final judgment of every person by Jesus Christ. His verdict lasts forever: everlasting life for those who belong to Him, and everlasting punishment for those who reject Him.

Who is the Judge?

Jesus Himself. The Father has given Him all authority to judge.

How did Jesus describe the final judgment?

Jesus used everyday pictures to show that at the end of the age, people will be divided into two groups:

  • Sheep and goats. When He comes in His glory, He will separate the nations “as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats” (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Wheat and tares. Good grain and weeds grow together until the harvest, and then they are separated (Matthew 13:24–30, 36–43).
  • The net. A net gathers every kind of fish, and then the good are kept and the bad thrown away (Matthew 13:47–50).

What will be judged?

  • Our words. “Every idle word that men shall speak, they shall give account thereof in the day of judgment” (Matthew 12:36).
  • How we treat others. “Inasmuch as ye have done it unto one of the least of these my brethren, ye have done it unto me” (Matthew 25:40).
  • Whether we really know Him. Calling Him “Lord, Lord” is not enough. To some He will say, “I never knew you: depart from me, ye that work iniquity” (Matthew 7:21–23).

What did Jesus say about hell?

Jesus spoke about hell more than anyone else in the Bible. He used the word Gehenna, the name of a valley outside Jerusalem where fires burned. He called it a place “where their worm dieth not, and the fire is not quenched” (Mark 9:44), and a place of “weeping and gnashing of teeth” (Matthew 8:12). He warned us because He does not want anyone to go there.

The good news: you do not have to fear judgment

Jesus came to save us from judgment, not to condemn us.

If you have turned to God in repentance and put your faith in Jesus, you have passed from death to life. That is where these six foundations began.

Recommended reading: Matthew 25:31–46, John 5:19–30, and Revelation 20:11–15.

Word Study · Growing in Christ

Neither barren nor unfruitful

God did not save you to sit still. He planted a new life in you, and it is meant to grow and bear fruit.

Peter gives new believers a simple plan for growth. Start with faith, and keep adding to it:

  1. Faith
  2. Virtue
  3. Knowledge
  4. Temperance
  5. Patience
  6. Godliness
  7. Brotherly kindness
  8. Charity (love)

Two Greek words in that verse show what God is keeping us from. Both begin with the little letter a-, which means “without,” the same way “a-” works in the English word atypical.

“Barren”

ἀργός

argos · say it ar-GOS

Strong’s number
G692
Built from
a- · without + ergon · work
Plain meaning
Without work: inactive, unemployed. By implication, lazy or useless. Translated barren, idle, slow.

“Unfruitful”

ἄκαρπος

akarpos · say it AK-ar-pos

Strong’s number
G175
Built from
a- · without + karpos · fruit
Plain meaning
Without fruit. Barren, whether a field that grows nothing or a life that shows nothing of God.

What this means for you

Remember the first foundation stone: we repent from dead works, things we did to earn God’s favor. Now Peter shows the other side. Once we belong to Jesus, our faith is not meant to sit without work or without fruit. As we add these qualities day by day, God makes our lives busy with His purposes and full of His fruit.

Questions & Answers

Has someone asked this already?

Search the questions other new believers have asked. If you don’t find yours, send it in.

How do I know I really repented?

If you are asking this question, God is already at work in your heart. Here is what the Word shows us:

  • Look at what happened inside. Did you see your sin differently, feel godly sorrow, and turn toward God? Those are the three things that happen when we repent.
  • Look for fruit over time. Real repentance shows in how we live, as the Holy Spirit grows His fruit in us. See fruit that fits repentance (Matthew 3:8; Galatians 5:22–23).
  • Rest on God’s promise, not your feelings. “If we confess our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive us our sins” (1 John 1:9). God wrote His Word “that ye may know that ye have eternal life” (1 John 5:13).
I’m a new believer. Where should I start reading the Bible?

Start in the New Testament, with the Gospel of John or the Gospel of Mark. John was written “that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name” (John 20:31).

Good books to start with

  • John: who Jesus is, why He came, and what it means to believe in Him.
  • Mark: a short, fast-moving account of Jesus’ life, miracles, ministry, death and resurrection.
  • Luke, then Acts: the life of Jesus, followed by the birth and spread of the early church.
  • James: a short, practical letter full of everyday wisdom for living as a Christian.

Where to go next

  • The letters, such as Ephesians, Philippians and Romans, to learn what we believe and how to live it out.
  • Psalms and Proverbs, a little each day, for prayer, comfort and wisdom.
  • Genesis, when you are ready for the Old Testament and the beginning of God’s story.

Tips for beginners

  • Read a little every day. Even one chapter is enough. Pray first, and focus on getting to know Jesus.
  • Don’t be troubled by hard parts. Ask the Holy Spirit to teach you (John 14:26), and send us your question.
  • Connect with a church. Study with others where you can ask questions and grow together. See churches we recommend.

This site is written for people of all ages who are new to Christ. We do not knowingly collect information from children under 13. If a child has sent us a message, a parent or guardian may ask us to delete it.

Your choices

You may ask us at any time to delete a message you sent, any reply, and any mailing address you gave us. Just send a note through the Ask & Pray form.
